Intellectual Property & Ethics

Copyright law, consent, licensing, DRM, ethical image manipulation, classification, and the difference between legal and ethical issues in multimedia.

⚠️ Exam tip — Legal vs Ethical

These are different things and the exam tests both. Legal = what is lawful or unlawful under Australian legislation. Ethical = what is morally right or wrong. Something can be legal but unethical (e.g. digitally slimming a model is lawful but may be considered harmful). Always address BOTH when a question asks for "legal and ethical" considerations.

Copyright & intellectual property

Copyright Law — Core Terms

CopyrightAutomatic legal right that protects original creative works the moment they are created — no registration needed in Australia. Lasts 70 years after the creator's death.
Copyright Act 1968Australian legislation governing copyright — protects music, film, software, images, written text, code, and multimedia works
Intellectual property (IP)The legal ownership of creations of the mind — includes copyright, trademarks, patents, and designs
Fair dealingLimited exceptions allowing use of copyrighted material without permission for specific purposes: research, criticism, news reporting, and education — NOT a blanket right to copy
Creative CommonsA licensing system allowing creators to share works with defined permissions — some rights reserved. Types: CC-BY (attribution only), CC-BY-SA (share alike), CC-BY-NC (non-commercial), CC0 (public domain)
PiracyIllegal copying, distribution or use of copyrighted material — criminal offence with fines and imprisonment under Australian law

Content Protection Methods

DRMDigital Rights Management — technological systems that prevent copying, sharing, or unauthorised use of digital content. Examples: streaming services that prevent downloading, locked ebook files.
WatermarkingEmbedding visible or invisible creator information into digital files — helps prove ownership and deter theft. Visible watermarks appear on stock photo previews.
SandboxingVideo player embedded within a webpage so the content is held in RAM only and cannot be easily saved — video does not create a permanent local file
Streaming protectionContent delivered in real time to RAM rather than stored on the device — makes copying more difficult than downloading a file
EncryptionScrambling content so it can only be read with the correct decryption key — used in DVD copy protection and streaming protocols
MetadataInformation embedded in digital files identifying the creator, date, and copyright status — can be stripped by editing software
Software licensing

Commercial Licences

ProprietarySoftware owned by a company — must be purchased. Source code not available. Adobe Photoshop, Microsoft Office.
SubscriptionMonthly or annual fee gives access to always-updated software — no large upfront cost. Adobe Creative Cloud, Microsoft 365.
Perpetual licenceOne-time purchase for a specific version of software — own it forever but no free upgrades
Multi-seat licenceAllows installation on a specified number of computers — required for businesses and schools

Free & Open Source

FreewareFree to use but copyright remains with creator — cannot be modified or sold. Source code usually not provided.
SharewareFree trial period with limited features — must pay after trial expires for full continued use
Open sourceSource code freely available — can be modified and redistributed. Examples: GIMP, Inkscape, Blender, VLC
Public domainNo copyright restrictions — can be used, modified and distributed freely by anyone. Copyright has expired or been waived.

Ethical Obligations

AttributionCrediting the original creator when using or referencing their work — ethical requirement even where not legally required
ConsentWritten permission required before filming/photographing individuals, especially children, in private or commercial settings
Privacy Act 1988Personal data must be collected lawfully, stored securely, and not used beyond its stated purpose — applies to images of individuals
DefamationPublishing false statements that damage a person's reputation — both civil and criminal liability can apply
ClassificationAustralian content ratings (G, PG, M, MA15+, R18+) — legal responsibility of the creator and distributor to correctly classify multimedia
Practice questions
Short Answer1 mark
Identify ONE method that can be used to protect video content from unauthorised copying.

DRM (Digital Rights Management) — technological protection systems that prevent users from copying, downloading or sharing digital video content. Examples include streaming platforms that hold content in RAM only during playback, or encrypted files that can only be played in authorised software.

Other acceptable answers

Watermarking, sandboxing/embedding the player in a webpage, streaming (content held in RAM only), encryption.

Short Answer4 marks
Provide a clear and reasoned comparison of TWO methods that can be used to protect video content from unauthorised copying.
PEEL Format
P
Two widely-used methods of video content protection are DRM-encrypted streaming and sandboxed web players — both aim to prevent unauthorised copying but differ significantly in their approach and effectiveness. 1 mark
E
DRM-encrypted streaming, as used by Netflix and Spotify, delivers video content directly to the device's RAM during playback. The content is encrypted and can only be decrypted by authorised playback software — it is never saved as a file on the user's device. This requires a subscription in the first place, adding a financial barrier to access. Developers continually update encryption standards as piracy tools emerge, making it highly effective when maintained. 1 mark
E
Sandboxing embeds the video player directly within a webpage so that the content is held in RAM and the video source URL is not readily accessible. This is less technically complex to implement than full DRM but is also easier to circumvent — tools exist that can extract the video stream URL or use screen recording. 1 mark
L
Both methods require ongoing updates to remain effective as circumvention tools evolve, and neither provides absolute protection. DRM offers significantly stronger protection for high-value commercial content, while sandboxing is better suited to lower-value content where complete security is less critical. 1 mark
Extended Response5 marks
Clearly describe the legal and ethical issues associated with including videos in multimedia presentations posted on social media platforms.
STEEL Format
S
Including videos in multimedia presentations shared on social platforms raises both legal and ethical obligations for the content creator, relating primarily to copyright, consent, and the potential impact of the content on individuals and audiences. 1 mark
T
From a legal perspective, any video included must be either original content, licensed for use, or qualify for a fair dealing exception under the Copyright Act 1968. Video footage often contains multiple layers of copyright — the cinematography, the music soundtrack, and any branded logos or artworks visible in frame — each requiring separate permission. Using copyrighted footage without authorisation exposes the creator to legal action including takedown notices, fines, and civil damages. 1 mark
E
Consent is also a legal requirement — individuals who are recognisable in the footage must provide consent for their image to be published, particularly when that footage is filmed on private property or commercial premises. Filming children requires parental consent, and failure to obtain this can have serious legal and reputational consequences. 1 mark
E
Ethical issues overlap with, but extend beyond, legal requirements. While content may be technically lawful to share, it could still be unethical — for example, posting footage that humiliates, embarrasses, or exposes an individual without their knowledge crosses ethical boundaries even if no law is technically broken. Content that portrays people in vulnerable or distressing situations should be handled with sensitivity regardless of legality. 1 mark
L
Responsible multimedia practice on social platforms therefore requires creators to seek permission before filming and publishing footage of others, to clear all copyright licences, to consider the impact of their content on the people depicted and the wider audience, and to correctly classify content that may not be suitable for all age groups. 1 mark
Extended Response5 marks
Describe the ethical issues involved in the development and use of multimedia products, using examples.
STEEL Format
S
The development and use of multimedia products raises a range of ethical issues spanning intellectual property, representation, classification, and the social impact of content on audiences. 1 mark
T
Multimedia designers have an ethical obligation to pay the appropriate licensing fees for all software and assets they use in production. Using unlicensed or pirated software not only violates the Copyright Act 1968 but deprives developers of the revenue needed to continue creating those tools — harming the entire creative industry. 1 mark
E
The use of manipulated or enhanced imagery in advertising raises ethical concerns about authenticity and consumer deception. Digitally altering the appearance of models to appear thinner or younger creates unrealistic beauty standards, which research links to body dissatisfaction, eating disorders and low self-esteem — particularly among young audiences. 1 mark
E
Ethical classification of multimedia content is also essential — content containing violence, sexual material or themes inappropriate for children must be correctly rated (G, M, MA15+, R18+) and distributed through age-appropriate channels. Distributing adult content without appropriate classification or access controls violates both the law and ethical obligations to protect vulnerable users. 1 mark
L
Ethical practice in multimedia requires ongoing critical reflection — creators must consider not only whether their work is legally compliant, but whether it respects the dignity and privacy of individuals depicted, accurately represents the people and events it portrays, and considers the potential harm that content may cause to audiences when widely distributed. 1 mark

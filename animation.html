Animation & Virtual Reality

2D and 3D animation techniques, motion capture, tweening, morphing — plus a full Virtual Reality deep dive covering VR/AR/MR, hardware, applications and ethics.

2D animation techniques

Traditional & Stop Motion

Cel animationIndividual drawings on transparent acetate sheets (cels) photographed sequentially — traditional Disney method
Stop motionPhysical objects photographed frame-by-frame — each photograph represents one frame of movement
ClaymationStop motion using clay or plasticine models that are reshaped between each frame — e.g. Wallace & Gromit
Cutout animationFlat characters or objects moved between frames — South Park began using this technique digitally

Keyframing & Tweening

KeyframeA specific frame where an object's state (position, size, colour, shape) is explicitly defined by the animator
TweeningSoftware automatically generates all frames BETWEEN two keyframes — "in-betweening". Saves huge amounts of animation work.
Motion tweenObject moves from one position to another across frames — position interpolated automatically
Shape tweenObject morphs from one shape to another across frames — both shape and colour interpolated
Frame rateSpeed of animation. 12fps = classic cartoon feel | 24fps = cinema standard | 60fps = smooth game animation

Path & Behaviour

Path animationObject automatically follows a defined curved or straight path between a start and end point
Behaviour animationObjects respond to programmed rules (gravity, collision detection, wind) rather than frame-by-frame instruction
LoopingAnimation plays continuously — last frame connects seamlessly to first frame
MorphingGradual, smooth transformation from one image to another — used in special effects and transitions
Onion skinningDisplaying transparent versions of previous frames to help animators position the next frame accurately
3D animation process

3D Production Pipeline

ModellingBuilding the 3D shape of objects using polygons (flat faces joined at edges and vertices), NURBS curves, or digital sculpting
WireframeSkeletal 3D representation showing only edges and vertices — used during modelling to see object structure
TexturingApplying surface material images (texture maps) to 3D models — gives appearance of wood, skin, fabric, metal etc.
UV mappingUnfolding a 3D surface into a flat 2D map so textures can be applied accurately to the model's surface
RiggingCreating a digital skeleton (armature) inside a 3D model so it can be posed and animated naturally
RenderingProcessing all 3D scene data (geometry, lighting, textures, shadows, reflections) to produce final 2D images. Very computationally intensive — can take hours per frame.
Ray tracingRendering technique simulating how light physically bounces — produces photorealistic shadows and reflections

Motion Capture (MoCap)

DefinitionRecording real human (or animal) movement and applying that data to a 3D digital character
Optical systemReflective markers placed on actor's joints, tracked by multiple infrared cameras — most common professional method
Inertial systemSmall sensor units (IMUs) worn on the body detecting movement via accelerometers and gyroscopes — no camera required
Facial captureTracking subtle facial muscle movements for realistic digital character expressions
AdvantagesRealistic, natural movement; faster than hand-keyframing; captures subtle expressions; data is reusable across multiple characters; reduces labour costs
DisadvantagesExpensive equipment and large capture volume required; cannot capture hair, cloth or fluid physics; requires cleanup in post; limited by actor's physical range
Virtual reality — deep dive

Real World

Physical reality — no digital overlay

Augmented Reality (AR)

Digital elements overlaid onto real world — e.g. Pokémon GO, IKEA app

Mixed Reality (MR)

Digital and physical objects interact — e.g. Microsoft HoloLens

Virtual Reality (VR)

Completely digital immersive environment — real world not visible

VR — Core Characteristics

ImmersionComplete replacement of the real visual environment with a 3D digital world — user cannot see the real world
InteractivityUser actions (head movement, controller input) change the virtual environment in real time — the defining feature that separates VR from passive 3D video
BelievabilityThe degree to which the experience feels real and convincing — affected by visual resolution, frame rate, latency, and audio accuracy
Real-time renderingThe environment must update and respond to user input with minimal latency (ideally under 20 milliseconds) — high latency causes motion sickness (cybersickness)
3D soundscapeSpatial audio that changes based on the user's head position and orientation — contributes greatly to the sense of presence in the virtual world
Haptic feedbackControllers or vests that vibrate or apply pressure — simulates the sensation of touching objects in the virtual world

VR Hardware & Software

HMDHead Mounted Display — a headset with one screen per eye creating stereoscopic 3D vision. Examples: Meta Quest, PlayStation VR, HTC Vive
6DoFSix Degrees of Freedom — tracks head movement in three axes (X/Y/Z for position) AND three rotations (pitch/yaw/roll). Essential for full immersion.
Field of View (FoV)The angle of vision provided by the headset. Human vision ≈ 220°. Consumer VR ≈ 90–120°. Wider FoV = more immersive.
Refresh rateHow many times per second the headset display updates. Minimum 72Hz recommended; 90Hz+ preferred to prevent motion sickness
Game engineSoftware used to create interactive VR experiences — Unity and Unreal Engine render 3D scenes in real time and handle physics and input
WalkthroughsPre-rendered or real-time 3D tours of environments — used in architecture, real estate, and education
Navigable scenesEnvironments where the user can freely move through and explore the 3D space using controllers or physical movement

VR Applications

  • Training simulators: Flight, surgery, military, firefighting — risks without real-world consequences
  • Architecture & design: Virtual walkthroughs of buildings before construction
  • Education: Virtual field trips, historical reconstructions, anatomy exploration
  • Healthcare: Phobia treatment (exposure therapy), pain management, rehabilitation
  • Entertainment: Immersive gaming, virtual concerts, theme parks
  • Product design: Virtual prototyping before physical manufacture

VR Advantages & Disadvantages

Advantages

  • Safe simulation of dangerous real-world scenarios
  • Repeatable without consequences or cost of real mistakes
  • Highly engaging and immersive learning environment
  • Can simulate physically impossible scenarios
  • Accessible to users who cannot travel to real locations

Disadvantages

  • Very high development and hardware costs
  • Motion sickness (cybersickness) from latency and visual-vestibular conflict
  • Social isolation — primarily a single-user experience
  • Cannot fully replicate tactile, smell, or temperature sensations
  • Requires regular content updates to remain useful

Extended Response4 marks
Explain the potential advantages of using motion capture technology in the production of a short animated film.
PEEL Format
P
Motion capture (MoCap) technology records the physical movements of human performers and applies that data to digital 3D characters, offering significant advantages over traditional hand-keyframed animation for a short animated film. 1 mark
E
The primary advantage is realism — MoCap captures the natural, fluid movement of a real human body, including subtle weight shifts, secondary motion, and micro-expressions, which are extraordinarily difficult and time-consuming to replicate through manual keyframing. This produces characters that feel convincing and relatable to audiences. 1 mark
E
MoCap also significantly reduces the time and labour required for complex action sequences. A ten-second fight scene that might take an animator weeks to keyframe can be captured in a single session with an actor. Furthermore, the captured motion data is reusable — the same performance can be applied to multiple different character rigs, adding long-term value to the investment. 1 mark
L
For a short animated film with a limited budget, selectively using MoCap for complex performance scenes — while relying on traditional techniques for simpler shots — can strike an effective balance between production cost, efficiency, and the visual quality needed to make the film engaging and marketable. 1 mark
Extended Response5 marks
Describe the characteristics of virtual reality technology and explain how it has been used in ONE industry application to benefit users.
STEEL Format
S
Virtual Reality (VR) is an immersive technology that replaces the user's real-world environment with a fully digital, interactive 3D environment experienced through a head-mounted display (HMD). 1 mark
T
The key characteristics of VR include immersion (complete visual replacement of the real world), interactivity (user actions change the environment in real time), and believability (the experience is convincing enough to produce genuine physiological and psychological responses). Technical components include HMDs providing stereoscopic vision, 6-degrees-of-freedom tracking, spatial audio, and haptic feedback controllers. 1 mark
E
In medical training, VR has been used to create surgical simulators where trainee surgeons can practise complex procedures in a fully immersive, risk-free environment. Systems such as Osso VR allow trainees to repeat procedures as many times as needed without any consequence of error — a patient is never at risk. 1 mark
E
Research has shown that surgeons who trained using VR simulators performed significantly better in real operating rooms, making fewer errors and completing procedures more efficiently than those trained using traditional methods alone. This directly benefits patient safety outcomes. 1 mark
L
VR therefore offers a transformative benefit in any high-stakes training environment — it provides the realism of real-world experience with the safety and repeatability of simulation, fundamentally changing how professionals in medicine, aviation, and emergency services develop and maintain critical skills. 1 mark
